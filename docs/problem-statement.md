# Problem Statement

## Background
Container ports are critical nodes in global supply chains, where hundreds of vessels, berths, quay cranes, and yard resources must be coordinated continuously. Vessel arrival times are uncertain, service durations vary, and disruptions such as weather, equipment failures, labor constraints, and sudden demand spikes can quickly overload port capacity.

The problem became highly visible during the 2021 Los Angeles–Long Beach congestion crisis, when vessel queues reached roughly 100 ships and delays at the ports propagated into wider global supply chains.

## The Problem
Port operations teams often make berth, crane, and vessel scheduling decisions using a combination of terminal systems, operational dashboards, spreadsheets, and manual judgment. These approaches are largely reactive: congestion is identified after vessels have already started waiting or berth utilization has become critical.

Port operators lack a unified system that can predict congestion before it occurs, determine the best allocation of berths and cranes under operational constraints, and generate an actionable 72-hour operations plan.
As a result, a delay involving one vessel can cascade into berth conflicts, crane imbalances, longer vessel waiting times, and additional downstream delays.

## Who is Affected
The primary users are:

Port operations managers responsible for resource allocation across terminals.
Shift supervisors who make real-time decisions about which vessel should use which berth and how cranes should be assigned.
Terminal planners who prepare vessel schedules and manage expected congestion.
Shipping lines and vessel operators whose vessels are delayed by berth queues.
Importers, exporters, and logistics providers affected by downstream schedule disruptions.
The most directly affected person is a port shift supervisor managing multiple arriving vessels and limited berths/cranes during a 24–72 hour planning window.

## Why It Matters
Port congestion creates both operational and economic costs.

During the 2021 Los Angeles–Long Beach disruption, around 70 container ships carrying approximately 432,909 TEU were reported waiting around the ports at one point. The Shanghai-to-destination journey through the San Pedro Bay ports also rose to approximately 62 days, more than twice its January 2020 level.

At the terminal level, congestion can result in:

Hours of vessel waiting time caused by berth conflicts.
Underutilized or poorly distributed cranes while other resources become bottlenecks.
Cascading schedule delays across subsequent vessels.
Higher operational and transportation costs.
Reduced predictability for the wider supply chain.

The key issue is not simply that congestion exists—it is that operators often discover the bottleneck too late to prevent it efficiently.

## Why Existing Solutions Fall Short
Ports already use vessel tracking systems, terminal operating systems, scheduling software, dashboards, and other operational tools. However, these tools often address individual parts of the problem rather than providing a complete predictive decision loop.

Existing workflows can typically answer:
"What is scheduled?"
"Where are the vessels?"
"What is happening right now?"

But the harder operational question is:
"What is likely to become congested over the next 6–72 hours, and what action should we take now to prevent it?"

Operators may still need to combine data manually, assess future congestion, test alternative assignments, and construct an operational plan themselves.
Our proposed solution closes this gap by combining:
Congestion Prediction → Optimization → Recommendations → 72-Hour Operations Plan
This transforms port management from a primarily reactive scheduling process into predictive and prescriptive decision-making.
